## Scope and how this review was assembled

This review sets the empirical context for a study that characterises the development of words **understood**, **spoken** and **signed** in children with Down syndrome (DS) from roughly 12 months to 8 years, using pooled parent-report checklist data and Bayesian trajectory models. Its organising questions follow the study: how vocabulary develops and why it matters; the gap between comprehension and production; the rate and shape of growth; the role of gesture and sign; the mechanisms that constrain word learning; individual variability; how vocabulary is measured; what interventions achieve; and how vocabulary growth has previously been modelled.

The review draws on two sources: a structured thematic search of **PubMed** (nine themes, each searched separately, with abstracts and identifiers retrieved for the most relevant and authoritative results), and the author's curated **Zotero** library. Every PubMed source cited was retrieved by an actual query and its PubMed identifier verified to resolve to the stated record; the set was screened for retractions, errata and expressions of concern, of which none were found among the cited works. The review cites **88 sources** in total. Inline citations are author–year; full references with DOIs (and PubMed IDs where applicable) are listed at the end.

## 1. Down syndrome and the centrality of vocabulary

Down syndrome is the most common genetic cause of intellectual disability, and language is consistently identified as among its most affected domains and a principal barrier to participation and inclusion (Abbeduto, Warren & Conners, 2007; Grieco et al., 2015). The behavioural language profile is reasonably consistent across individuals despite wide variation in its severity: receptive language is typically stronger than expressive language, and phonology and syntax are areas of particular weakness, set against a background of hearing, oral-motor, cognitive and prelinguistic-communication characteristics specific to the syndrome (Martin et al., 2009; Chapman & Kay-Raining Bird, 2011; Abbeduto et al., 2020). Across the lifespan, nonverbal skills broadly track mental age while verbal — and especially expressive — abilities fall progressively further behind, with delays becoming pronounced by school age (Grieco et al., 2015).

Vocabulary occupies a central place in this profile, both as a relative weakness and as a developmental foundation. A meta-analysis of language and verbal short-term memory found broad language deficits in DS that are _not_ restricted to expressive measures, accompanied by a disproportionate verbal short-term memory impairment relative to nonverbal-mental-age-matched peers (Næss et al., 2011b). Vocabulary knowledge in turn scaffolds later achievements: it predicts decoding and reading ability over and above phonological awareness (Næss et al., 2011a), constrains the growth of reading skills longitudinally (Hulme et al., 2012), and — through receptive vocabulary and, specifically, verb knowledge — supports the emergence of syntax (Loveall et al., 2016). Not every communicative domain is impaired: joint attention is a relative strength in DS, commensurate with developmental level and comparable to typically developing (TD) children (Hahn et al., 2018), which is part of why early gesture and sign can be productively recruited (Section 5). Because expressive vocabulary is both disproportionately affected and foundational, it — together with the relationship between understanding and saying — is the natural primary target for a study aiming to provide interpretable developmental expectations.

## 2. Comprehension ahead of production: the receptive–expressive asymmetry

A defining and much-replicated feature of language in DS is that comprehension runs ahead of production (Chapman & Hesketh, 2001; Martin et al., 2009). The asymmetry is not simply a by-product of global cognitive delay. When infants with DS are matched to TD infants on **gains** in nonverbal mental ability, the apparent expressive delay largely disappears and a receptive-language advantage emerges (Mason-Apps et al., 2020), indicating that the comprehension–production discrepancy reflects something specific to producing words rather than to general developmental level. This is precisely the structure that motivates modelling words understood and words spoken jointly and summarising their relationship as a production ratio.

Several lines of evidence localise the difficulty to expressive output. Children with DS show intact single-word comprehension and can fast-map novel words from event contexts much as TD children do (Chapman, Bird & Schwartz, 1990), yet show pronounced weaknesses in verb comprehension-to-use and in sentence production (Michael, Ratner & Newman, 2012); early in development they substitute gesture for speech while vocal comprehension and gestural production advance together (Caselli et al., 1998). The receptive-over-expressive profile is reliably captured by parent-report inventories alongside standardised tests, and the predictors of the two vocabularies overlap with, without being identical to, those in TD children (Deckers et al., 2017).

Cross-syndrome comparison sharpens the interpretation. Toddlers with Williams syndrome (WS) tend to have larger expressive vocabularies and a markedly smaller comprehension–production gap than chronological-age-matched children with DS (Mervis & Robinson, 2000; Vicari et al., 2002; Volterra et al., 2003); however, vocabulary-matched designs suggest that part of the apparent WS "advantage" reflects the severity of the **production** deficit in DS rather than genuine sparing in WS. Recent computational and CDI-based work confirms that the comprehension–production asymmetry is selectively _reduced_ in WS but _preserved_ in DS and fragile X syndrome, framing the DS profile as one pole of a syndrome-specific spectrum of receptive–expressive dissociation; in that study (Communicative Development Inventory data from 67 children with WS, 27 with DS, 15 with fragile X and 1,210 TD children) a self-organising-map model attributed the WS pattern to atypical visual processing (D'Souza et al., 2026). The implication for the present work is that the size and shape of the comprehension–production gap is itself an informative, syndrome-characterising quantity worth estimating directly.

## 3. Trajectories, rate of word learning, and the vocabulary spurt

Parent-report studies converge on a picture of vocabulary growth in DS that is delayed and slowed relative to TD children but broadly similar in shape once children are aligned by vocabulary size rather than by age. Early growth follows exponential or logistic curves, and large normative parent-report data place the spoken vocabulary of children with DS at 3–4 years at a level comparable to TD children around 16–20 months (Berglund, Eriksson & Johansson, 2001). Longitudinal and cross-sectional CDI data show a marked increase in between-child variability from about 36 months and confirm that lexical development lags even behind cognitive level (Zampini & D'Odorico, 2013). When children are matched on vocabulary size, the **composition** of early vocabulary in DS largely mirrors TD — with somewhat more nouns — implying broadly comparable early word-learning strategies (Checa, Galeote & Soto, 2016).

Milestone data give concrete anchors. In an early longitudinal study using parental records, children with DS reached the 10-word stage at a mean of about 27 months (a delay of roughly a year), a vocabulary "explosion" — where it occurred — at about 30 months with a mean vocabulary near 24 words, and two-word combinations at about 37 months (a delay of roughly 18 months); critically, some children showed no clear spurt, and individual variability was very wide (Oliver & Buckley, 1994). Longitudinal monthly data likewise identify distinct expressive-vocabulary growth patterns — marginal growth, growth without a spurt, and growth with a spurt — together with shifting modality profiles (from gesture-only toward gesture-plus-speech), reinforcing that a vocabulary spurt is far from universal in DS (Kaat-van den Os et al., 2017). The rate (slope) of growth is shaped by adaptive functioning, receptive skill and environmental factors, with predictors resembling those operating in TD children, and it slows further under adversity such as parental depression (Deckers et al., 2017; D'Souza et al., 2020). Longitudinal and cross-syndrome work indicates that children with DS show less change over time than TD children while preserving the typical comprehension-over-production asymmetry, and — importantly for any modelling effort — that individual trajectories are poorly predicted by diagnosis alone (Martin et al., 2013; Foster-Cohen, Macrae & Newbury, 2023). DS-adapted CDIs provide validated instruments for estimating these trajectories (Galeote et al., 2016).

## 4. Beyond speech: gesture and manual sign

A consistent literature documents a "gesture advantage" in young children with DS. Matched to TD children on word comprehension, children with DS show comparable verbal production but significantly stronger gesture production, and gestural development tracks lexical comprehension while spoken production lags behind (Caselli et al., 1998; Iverson, Longobardi & Caselli, 2003). The advantage is not uniform across gesture types: deictic/pointing gestures are actually reduced relative to TD peers, whereas iconic gesture and taught "baby signs" are relative assets, and in a naming task children with DS produce more iconic gestures that often convey the target concept their unintelligible speech does not (Stefanini, Caselli & Volterra, 2007).

Whether early gesture and sign feed later spoken vocabulary has direct bearing on how a "total expressive" vocabulary should be conceived. Longitudinal evidence shows that early gesture provides "a helping hand" to spoken vocabulary in DS as in TD and autistic children (Özçalışkan et al., 2017), but that it is specifically **taught baby signs**, not spontaneous deictic gesture, that predict later spoken vocabulary in DS (Özçalışkan et al., 2015). Modality therefore matters for both measurement and intervention: parent-report CDIs that include signed as well as spoken items are valid and capture expressive vocabulary that speech-only counts miss (Deckers et al., 2016); oral and gestural vocabularies trade off, with children who say more words gesturing less (a balance even modulated by sleep quality; Arias-Trejo et al., 2020); novel words are learned from combined sign-plus-speech presentation although the spoken form is preferentially produced (Bird et al., 2000); caregivers naturally combine gesture and speech in their input (Iverson et al., 2006); and embedding key-word signing in shared book reading increases children's sign use, attention and initiation (Frizelle et al., 2022). Consistent with sign and gesture forming part of a single expressive repertoire, a matched comparison found that children with DS produced spoken words at a level similar to TD children and gesture at a superior level, such that combining words and gestures left the two groups performing almost equally (Galeote et al., 2008). A recent systematic mini-review confirms that gestures and baby signs are among the significant early predictors of later language outcomes in DS (Filipe et al., 2022). These findings justify treating signing as a distinct production channel and modelling a total expressive vocabulary that combines speech and sign.

## 5. What constrains word learning: verbal memory, hearing, and speech-motor skills

Three interlocking mechanisms are repeatedly implicated in constraining word learning — and especially expressive vocabulary — in DS.

**Verbal/phonological short-term memory.** A robust, well-replicated weakness in verbal short-term memory accompanies the broader language deficit and is disproportionate relative to nonverbal-mental-age-matched peers (Næss et al., 2011b). The deficit appears specific to memory for verbal material rather than reducible to auditory or speech-motor confounds (Jarrold, Baddeley & Phillips, 2002), persists across development, and tracks vocabulary growth over time (Godfrey & Lee, 2018; Næss et al., 2015). Nonword repetition, a common index of phonological memory, predicts vocabulary and comprehension, though the nonword-repetition–vocabulary link is weaker or absent in DS than in TD children, pointing to additional phonological and speech-discrimination difficulties (Laws, 1998; Loveall et al., 2016; Alsaeed et al., 2024).

**Hearing.** Hearing loss and otitis media with effusion are highly prevalent in DS (Kanamori et al., 2000), and early or fluctuating hearing loss is associated with poorer language outcomes — an association that is clearer when hearing history is measured cumulatively rather than concurrently (Laws & Hall, 2014). Co-occurring medical conditions more generally are linked to cognition, language and behaviour in DS (Schworer et al., 2022).

**Speech-motor skills.** Motor-speech disorders, including childhood apraxia of speech and dysarthria, are common in DS, reduce intelligibility, and widen the gap between what children know and what they can say (Kumin, 2006; Cleland et al., 2010; Wilson et al., 2019); articulation-focused intervention can improve speech production (Wood et al., 2018). Together these correlates help explain why expressive vocabulary lags comprehension and why production ratios are depressed in DS — and they caution that observed "production" partly reflects constraints on output that are distinct from underlying word knowledge.

## 6. Individual variability and its predictors

Between-child variability in DS vocabulary is wide and grows with development (Zampini & D'Odorico, 2013), and it is structured rather than mere noise. Cluster and latent-profile analyses identify multiple distinct cognitive and expressive-language subgroups within DS (Karmiloff-Smith et al., 2016; Channell et al., 2021; Onnivello et al., 2022; Spellun et al., 2023), and individual vocabulary trajectories are poorly predicted by diagnosis alone (Foster-Cohen, Macrae & Newbury, 2023). The field has increasingly sought to define expressive-language benchmarks that acknowledge this heterogeneity (Thurman et al., 2022).

A recognisable, partly modifiable set of predictors recurs. Expressive vocabulary growth is driven by receptive vocabulary, verbal/auditory short-term memory, adaptive functioning, attention, and oral-motor and phonological-awareness skills, with several of these acting more strongly in DS than in TD children (Næss et al., 2021; Deckers et al., 2017; Estigarribia, Martin & Roberts, 2012). A systematic mini-review adds maternal education, child communicative intent, early vocalisations and gestures/baby signs to this list (Filipe et al., 2022). Nonverbal mental ability is a key alignment variable: equating it reveals comparable expressive growth and a relative receptive advantage in DS infants, bearing directly on the comprehension–production gap (Mason-Apps et al., 2018, 2020). Environmental input is also implicated — caregivers of children with DS tend to provide simplified, more telegraphic, label- and question-rich input during interaction and book reading (Lorang, Venker & Sterling, 2019; Barton-Hulsey et al., 2020) — and exploratory behaviour and other early non-linguistic skills index later developmental functioning (Fidler et al., 2019). The breadth and heterogeneity of these predictors is itself an argument for hierarchical models that allow each child their own level and rate while partially pooling information across children and studies.

## 7. Measuring vocabulary: parent report, the CDI, and normative comparison

The MacArthur–Bates Communicative Development Inventories (CDIs) are the dominant instrument for measuring early vocabulary in DS, and their validity in this population is well established. The foundational validation reported strong concurrent correlations (r ≈ 0.70–0.82) between CDI scores and laboratory vocabulary measures in children with DS and mental-age-matched TD controls, with significant predictive validity over time (Miller, Sedey & Miolo, 1995). Subsequent syndrome- and language-specific adaptations corroborate this — the Spanish CDI for children with DS (Galeote et al., 2016) and the Dutch N-CDI (Deckers et al., 2016) demonstrate validity and reliability for both spoken and signed expressive vocabulary, the latter underlining the need to capture the signed modality that standard forms omit. Short and screening forms are being developed and validated to reduce respondent burden (Mayor & Mani, 2019; Filipe et al., 2025).

Normative and methodological infrastructure for cross-study comparison is provided by **Wordbank**, an open, cross-linguistic repository of CDI data that enables vocabulary-size and word-type comparisons against TD reference data (Frank et al., 2016); large normative parent-report datasets show children with DS broadly tracking TD trajectories while lagging on grammar and pragmatics at matched vocabulary size (Berglund, Eriksson & Johansson, 2001), and modality- and word-type composition has been charted in emerging DS vocabularies (Foster-Cohen et al., 2022). The most recent systematic review of CDI use across DS and other neurodevelopmental conditions catalogues the methodological caveats most relevant to pooling and modelling: reporting biases, cross-linguistic adaptation, form ceilings and screening cut-offs, and the limits of single-timepoint assessment (Laudańska et al., 2026). These caveats motivate careful harmonisation across instruments and explicit, calibrated treatment of measurement and uncertainty — and they bear directly on a known hazard, that the "Words & Sentences" CDI form measures production only, so its comprehension items cannot be treated as a valid receptive measure.

## 8. Intervention and the need for interpretable expectations

The intervention literature is consistent on two points: children with DS do respond to language teaching, but gains concentrate on directly taught items with weak transfer to untrained skills. A systematic review and meta-analysis reported a large overall effect of language interventions (g ≈ 1.01) but rare generalisation and a moderate-to-high risk of bias across studies (Smith, Hokstad & Næss, 2020); an earlier communication-intervention meta-analysis reached similar conclusions (Neil & Jones, 2016); and a landmark reading-and-language randomised controlled trial found improvements largest for taught vocabulary that did not generalise (Burgoyne et al., 2012). Parent-implemented approaches are central but thinly evidenced — a Cochrane review found only three small, very-low-quality trials of parent-mediated DS interventions (O'Toole et al., 2018) — although parent-delivered Enhanced Milieu Teaching has produced durable strategy use and large effects in mixed intellectual-disability samples (Kaiser & Roberts, 2012). Recent family-co-developed, service-embedded and scalable programmes signal a shift toward early, multimodal, family-centred delivery: the ASCEND infant social-communication feasibility trial (Stojanovik et al., 2022), PACT-DS (Burgoyne et al., 2023), an online language-through-music programme whose dose–response depended on baseline vocabulary level (Frizelle et al., 2024), and augmentative-and-alternative-communication / aided-language modelling work (Quinn, Kaiser & Ledford, 2019; Romski et al., 2023). Outcomes are shaped by predictors such as younger age, baseline receptive language and the home-literacy environment (Dulin, Loveall & Mattie, 2023).

Two threads make the applied case for the present study. First, the limited transfer of intervention gains means that **realistic, interpretable expectations** — what range of vocabulary a child of a given age or stage might be expected to understand and say, and how fast it typically grows — are themselves a clinically valuable output, needed to set goals, gauge progress, and identify children who may need additional support (Thurman et al., 2022). Second, families and practitioners have explicitly called for research focused on practically useful, accessible, evidence-based information of exactly this kind (Cristescu et al., 2024). Interpretable, age- and vocabulary-referenced trajectory estimates with honest uncertainty respond directly to that call.

## 9. Modelling vocabulary growth: prior approaches and the gap addressed here

Quantitative modelling of vocabulary has largely used parametric and multilevel growth-curve methods applied to parent-report data. Normative percentile trajectories and the extensive between-child variability that any model must accommodate were established for TD children by Fenson et al. (1994), and Wordbank now makes pooled, cross-study CDI analysis feasible at scale (Frank et al., 2016). Recent work fits interpretable parametric trajectories directly to such data — most relevantly, Gompertz growth curves applied to longitudinal CDI word production, yielding "growth rate" and "age at maximum growth" parameters and including a clinical comparison (Day et al., 2025). In DS specifically, growth-curve and hierarchical-linear-modelling approaches have estimated individual intercepts and slopes and characterised large variability and trajectory turning points (Chapman, Hesketh & Kistler, 2002; Couzens, Cuskelly & Haynes, 2011), and mixed-effects modelling across neurodevelopmental diagnoses shows that individual DS trajectories are poorly predicted by diagnosis alone — reinforcing the case for hierarchical models with subject-level effects (Foster-Cohen, Macrae & Newbury, 2023). Bayesian and item-response-theory frameworks have so far been applied mainly to shorten or impute CDI scores by combining priors with repository data (Mayor & Mani, 2019; Chai, Lo & Mayor, 2020), while cross-syndrome computational work has modelled the comprehension–production gap and its "typicality" against Wordbank references, including DS (McDaniel et al., 2018; D'Souza et al., 2026).

Two methodological strands complete the picture. **Integrative data analysis** — the principled pooling and joint modelling of several independent datasets — offers a route to the statistical power and generalisability that single small DS samples lack, while modelling between-study heterogeneity explicitly (Curran et al., 2017). And the broader case for Bayesian inference is that it yields full posterior distributions and directly interpretable probability statements about quantities of interest, which suits small, heterogeneous samples and the goal of communicating expectations with calibrated uncertainty.

The gap is therefore clear. Prior studies have variously: described DS vocabulary trajectories and milestones; documented the comprehension–production asymmetry and the gesture/sign advantage; fitted growth curves or hierarchical linear models to single samples; and used Bayesian/IRT tools to shorten instruments. What has not been done is to combine **integrative data analysis across multiple pooled, independent DS samples** with a **full Bayesian trajectory model** that jointly estimates words understood, spoken and signed; the production ratio and the comprehension–production gap; the contribution of sign to total expressive vocabulary; and individual variability — all with calibrated uncertainty and reported in interpretable, age- and vocabulary-referenced terms. Supplying that is the contribution of the present study.

## 10. Summary and rationale for the present study

The literature supports a coherent rationale. Vocabulary is a disproportionately affected yet foundational aspect of development in DS (Section 1); understanding reliably outpaces saying, in a way that is specific to production rather than explained by general delay, and that differs informatively from other syndromes (Section 2); growth is delayed and slowed but broadly typical in shape once aligned by vocabulary size, with wide and structured individual variability (Sections 3, 6); gesture and sign are genuine, partly teachable expressive channels that a complete account of "expressive vocabulary" should include (Section 4); verbal memory, hearing and speech-motor constraints help explain the depressed production ratio (Section 5); the CDI provides validated measurement but with caveats that demand careful harmonisation and explicit uncertainty (Section 7); intervention works but transfers poorly, so trustworthy developmental expectations are themselves valuable and are what families ask for (Section 8); and prior modelling, though informative, has not delivered a pooled, fully Bayesian, multi-modality trajectory model of the kind this study provides (Section 9). The present study addresses these threads together by estimating interpretable trajectories of words understood, spoken and signed — and the relationships among them — to inform expectations, teaching and intervention for children with Down syndrome.
