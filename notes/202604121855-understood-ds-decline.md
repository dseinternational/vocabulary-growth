## Summary

Models VG02 (univariate: age -> understood, DS) and VG05 (bivariate: age -> understood + spoken, DS) both show an apparent decline in expected words understood between approximately 40 and 60 months, with the median posterior estimate dropping from ~290 words at 42 months to ~273 at 54 months before recovering. The VG05 learning rate plot shows the median estimated gain per month crossing below zero in this window. The VG05 understood-vs-spoken plot shows a corresponding "leftward hook" where comprehension retreats while production continues to rise.

This investigation concludes that the decline is a **data composition artefact** (a form of Simpson's paradox), not a real developmental phenomenon or a model defect.

## Why the model fits a dip

The GP (Gaussian process) component of the model has sufficient flexibility (length-scale prior mapped to 6--18 months, amplitude `eta_sigma = 0.4` on the logit scale) to fit localised departures from the linear trend. With only 36 understood observations in the 50--60 month window -- and those observations drawn predominantly from lower-scoring studies -- the GP fits a dip to track the observed data. The model is doing exactly what it should: fitting the data it sees. The problem is that the data in this window is not representative of the same population mixture as the adjacent windows.

## Why this is not a real developmental phenomenon

1. **Biological implausibility**: A population-level decline in receptive vocabulary between 40 and 60 months would be inconsistent with the broader developmental literature for children with Down syndrome. Comprehension is expected to increase monotonically at the population level, even if the rate of growth slows.

2. **Simpson's paradox**: The decline disappears when the compositional shift is accounted for. Within each study, understood scores at 50--55 months are consistent with or higher than scores at 40--45 months for the same study.

3. **Consistency across models**: Both VG02 (univariate) and VG05 (bivariate) produce nearly identical dips, confirming this is a property of the understood data, not a consequence of bivariate coupling or a specific model formulation.

## Implications

### For reporting

The dip should be noted as a data limitation in model reports for VG02 and VG05. The compositional shift in contributing studies between 40 and 60 months means that posterior estimates in this age range should be interpreted with caution.

### For model development

Several approaches could mitigate this artefact:

- **Study-level random intercepts or effects**: Allowing the model to account for systematic differences between studies would reduce the impact of compositional shifts. This is the most principled solution but adds model complexity.
- **GP hyper-prior sensitivity**: Tightening `eta_sigma` (e.g., from 0.4 to 0.2) would reduce the GP's ability to fit localised dips. If the dip disappears with a tighter amplitude prior, that confirms it is noise-driven rather than signal. This is a useful diagnostic but not a fix.
- **Monotonicity constraints**: Imposing a soft or hard constraint that expected comprehension is non-decreasing with age would prevent the dip. This encodes a strong domain assumption and should only be considered if scientifically justified.

### For data collection

The root cause is missing understood data in the 40--60+ month age range, particularly from Studies 1 and 5 which recorded spoken data without corresponding understood data. Future data collection or harmonisation efforts should prioritise complete understood + spoken pairs, especially in the 40--70 month range for children with Down syndrome.
