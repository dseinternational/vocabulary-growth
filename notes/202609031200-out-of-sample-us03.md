A new Down syndrome source arrived on 2026-09-03: Fidler et al., Project CAPEabilities and Project EXPO, 186 children on the 396-item English CDI Words and Gestures form, up to two visits each. Before it is registered in `prepare_data.py` — which would change every Down syndrome prepared-frame hash and stale all thirteen Down syndrome fits — the published VG20 posterior was used to predict it. Nothing was refitted and the file stays outside `data/` until this closes.

Three rows exceed the 396-item form and were excluded on the user's instruction (`id` 113 v2, `id` 130 v2, `id` 3 v1; the source's own documentation flags all three). That leaves **287 rows, 183 children, 104 of them with two usable visits**. Thirty rows record zero comprehension and two sit at the form's ceiling of 396.

**The design has one property everything below has to be read through: age and visit are the same variable.** Every first visit falls at 17–25 months (median 20) and every second visit at 29–35 (median 31), apart from three first visits at 62–80 months from a structurally distinct sub-sample. A difference between age bands is therefore a difference between visits, and a difference between the 17–19 and 20–25 bands is a difference between children, not a change within any child.

## This version

This is the fourth version of the note. Beyond the figures it replaces, it corrects three things a reader of the earlier versions will have taken away.

1. The earlier headline, that the study's departure from the comprehension curve "grows with age, from about -0.17 at 17 months to about -1.05 at 34", was a linear reading of four band estimates. The month-by-month profile shows a **step** at 20 months, from on-the-curve to about -0.7, flat thereafter, with the last band's -1.04 carried by six children at 34–35 months. Within the 104 children seen twice the departure does widen between visits, by about 0.22 logits, which is a smaller and better-founded statement than the one it replaces.
2. The band-to-band spread of 0.88 that was quoted against a simulated null of at most 0.58 depends on isolating those twenty 33–35-month rows as their own band, a choice made after seeing the data. Under three other reasonable bandings the same study's spread is 0.54, 0.54 and 0.18. The test is now reported for every banding, alongside a likelihood-ratio statistic that does not depend on which one band is most extreme, and alongside the within-child change, each against its own null.
3. The analyses behind the null distributions, the age profile and the model comparison were run as inline commands and never saved. They are now the `checks` script, and the note's figures come from its output files.

Two smaller corrections are in the defect list at the end: the within-child predictive carried one study offset per posterior draw rather than one per candidate, and the earlier log-predictive comparison applied the child-effect correlation to the combined study and child effect.

## Headline

**The conversion model transfers to an unseen study. The comprehension model does not.** The study sits on the fitted comprehension curve at 18–19 months and about 0.7 logits below it from 20 months on; within children the departure widens by a further 0.2 logits between the two visits. No registered model has a term for a study-level departure that changes with age, so none of the six scored absorbs it. Whether the shape belongs to the curve or to this cohort's recruitment cannot be settled from one study, and the note says which reading each piece of evidence favours.

The evidence that the departure is not a constant offset: under every banding that separates the 17–19-month rows from the 20–25-month rows, none of 100 studies simulated from the model — each with a constant offset by construction — reaches the real study's band-to-band spread or its likelihood-ratio statistic (0 of 100 on each of four such bandings). The first-visit-against-second-visit contrast on its own is unremarkable (9 of 100 reach it), and the widening within children is borderline (5 of 100 reach -0.22). **What the model cannot produce is the step at 20 months within the first visit, not the change between visits.**

## Is the departure constant? The null distributions

A study simulated from the model has a constant offset by construction: one posterior draw is the truth, a study offset is drawn at the fitted between-study scale, child effects are drawn once per child and shared across their two visits, counts are censored at the form, and the result carries `us_03`'s own ages, children and visit structure. Whatever variation across bands or between visits such a study shows is estimation noise, and that is the reference for the same statistics on the real study. `checks null` ran 100 of them, as four parts of 25 with different seeds, pooled by `checks null-summary`.

Three statistics, each on the real study and on the simulated ones. The **spread** is the largest band offset minus the smallest. The **likelihood ratio** is twice the gain in profile log-likelihood from giving each band its own offset instead of one shared offset, which does not depend on which single band is extreme; it is only defined for models whose child effect is constant in age, which VG20's is. The **paired change** is the second-visit offset minus the first-visit offset on the 104 children seen twice.

Three things follow. First, the earlier claim survives in substance but not in its margin: with 30 simulated studies the primary banding's null reached 0.58 and the real 0.88 looked far outside it; with 100 it reaches 0.82. The likelihood ratio is the better guide, 20.4 against a null maximum of 15.8, and it says the same under every banding that keeps 17–19 months separate from 20–25. Second, that is the contrast doing the work: the 17–19 band sits 0.54 above the 20–25 band in the real study, where across 100 simulated studies the same difference runs from -0.44 to +0.32 and never reaches it. When the two first-visit bands are merged, so that only the first visit is compared with the second, the real study is unremarkable, at 9 of 100. Third, the within-child widening of 0.22 is at the edge of what a constant-offset study produces: the null's paired change has a standard deviation of 0.09, and 5 of 100 reached -0.22. It is evidence, not proof, that the departure grows as these children age.

The simulated studies also say what the estimator's own noise is on this design: across 100 studies the whole-study offset was recovered with a standard deviation of 0.084 and a largest error of 0.244, about twice what the profile interval implies, which is the cost of treating 287 rows as 287 children.

## What the coverage null says, and what it does not

An earlier version of this note claimed the comprehension coverage was outside the model's null distribution, on the strength of 24 simulated studies. That claim was withdrawn in the third version after a second simulation of 30 produced four studies at or below it, and the pooled 100 say the same: 2 of 100 simulated studies covered at or below 0.801, and both carried offsets of 1.1 to 1.3 in absolute value, twice `us_03`'s. Coverage in these simulations is mostly a function of how far the study's offset sits from zero, at a correlation of -0.78 between the offset's absolute value and the coverage, and the simulated studies whose offsets were within 0.15 of `us_03`'s -0.56 covered 0.861 to 0.868 (four studies, offsets -0.51 to -0.61), with median PITs of 0.24 to 0.31 against `us_03`'s 0.351. So `us_03` is covered somewhat worse than the model produces for a study at its own offset, but not remarkably so. The coverage figure is a symptom; the shape above is the finding.

## Two alternative explanations, tested and rejected, and one that cannot be

**The 396-item form.** The models score every count against an 810-item reference, so a study wholly on the shortest form could read low for that reason alone — the `dse-native-only` question. It does not explain this. Restricting to the 180 rows where under 5% of the predictive mass sits above 396 gives 89% coverage of 0.800 and median PIT 0.351, against 0.804 and 0.351 for the other 107 rows, which is no difference at all. More directly, `us_01` is the same 396-item Words and Gestures form in the fitted pool and carries a fitted offset of **+0.15**, and across the fourteen fitted studies the correlation between offset and form length is only +0.27.

**A young sample outrunning the curve's calibration.** If the curve were poorly calibrated where the pool is sparse, the youngest rows would be the worst predicted. They are the best.

**Recruitment.** A study that enrolled children by developmental stage rather than by age, or in waves that differed, would show exactly a step within the first visit and no age gradient inside it. So would a population curve that rises too steeply between 18 and 24 months. The two are not separable from one study whose first visits all fall in that window, and the source carries no covariate that could separate them. The refit will: if the curve moves, it was the curve.

**The correlation does not pay out of sample.** On the task built for it, VG20 is worse than every other model by about 1.8 nats, none of the differences distinguishable from zero. It buys nothing on production given both outcomes either against the constant-effect models, and the two age-varying child structures are better there by 4 to 6 nats, at z of about -1.7. And it costs something on comprehension: giving VG20 the visit-1 production count as well as the comprehension count makes its visit-2 comprehension prediction **worse**, by 4.1 nats (z -1.8), where for VG09 and VG22 the two conditionings are indistinguishable. The loss concentrates in the fifteen children who produced a fifth or more of what they understood at visit 1, at 0.13 nats each against 0.03 for the rest (`oos_lpd_comparison.csv`, the conditioning-contrast rows). That is the correlation doing what it is fitted to do — reading a high production ratio as a sign of high comprehension standing — on a study whose ratios are high because of its own conversion offset.

This does not show the correlation is wrong. Parameter recovery confirmed it is identified, and it is the estimand behind the comparison claims about which children convert more. It shows that on 104 unseen children it buys no predictive gain, and that where its two halves point in opposite directions, as this study's offsets do, it transfers a study effect into child predictions. VG20 is the best of the five at predicting visit-2 comprehension from visit-1 comprehension, by 0.04 to 0.17 nats per child; that reflects its comprehension curve and scales rather than the correlation, which does not enter that prediction. With 104 children a per-child difference below about 0.05 nats is invisible here.

## Against the pass criteria set in advance

Issue #288 fixed these before the data were seen.

- **89% coverage within about ±4 points of nominal** — **not met**. Understood is 9 points low at 0.801. Spoken is 6 points high marginally at 0.951 and within tolerance conditionally at 0.856.
- **Study offsets inside the fitted spread** — **met**, both quantities.
- **Conditional coverage no worse than marginal** — **met for spoken** (0.856 conditionally against 0.951 marginally, so closer to nominal), **not met for understood** (0.750 against 0.801).
- **No systematic sign to predicted minus observed** — **not met for understood**: the model predicts too high from 20 months on, and more so at the second visit than the first for the same children.

## What this means for the ingestion refit

The model has a constant per-study offset on comprehension. This study's departure is not constant: zero at 18–19 months, about -0.7 from 20 months, and 0.2 wider at the second visit than the first within children. Two readings remain open and this study alone cannot separate them: the population comprehension curve rises too steeply between 18 and 24 months, or study effects are genuinely age-varying and the model needs a term for that. The first is testable once this study is in the pool, since the curve will move; the second is a structural question and connects to Proposal A1's age-varying scale, already registered as a sensitivity variant on VG10. A third possibility, that the step is this cohort's recruitment, is not a modelling question at all and is the one to put to the data providers.

Ingesting the study will pull the comprehension curve down between 20 and 35 months and widen the between-study scale. Both are wanted. Neither was predictable from the fitted pool, which is the argument the out-of-sample test was built to make.

## Defects found in my own work, all fixed

1. **The offset estimator was wrong.** It first took the mean of each row's continuity-corrected empirical logit residual and returned **-0.96, outside the fitted range**, which would have been reported as a failure of the reference-child estimand. Thirty of 287 rows record zero comprehension; `logit` is undefined at zero and the `+0.5` correction places those rows near -5.5, supplying more than half the displacement. It is now a profile likelihood with the child effect integrated out by Gauss-Hermite quadrature. **Any summary that logit-transforms individual counts is unusable on a source with a floor, and this pool has several.**
2. **No missing-outcome guard.** The fitted Down syndrome pool carries 448 spoken-only rows on the fallback likelihood. The first calibration control ran on all 1,424 and returned 0.631 coverage, because a comparison against `NaN` reads as a miss. Restricted to the 972 complete rows the same code returns 0.92. The script now drops incomplete rows and reports the count. The `us_03` frame has none, so its numbers never changed.
3. **A mislabelled diagnostic column.** `observed_t1` in the within-child output selected its column by testing whether `"understood"` appeared in the outcome name, which is true of `spoken_given_observed_understood`. Reporting only; no coverage figure was affected.
4. **An overstated null-distribution claim.** The first version reported that `us_03`'s comprehension coverage was outside a null of 24 simulated studies. Larger simulations put several studies at or below it. **A simulation-based null quoted as "outside all N" needs N large enough that the tail is populated, and needs to test the specific statistic the finding is about.**
5. **The within-child candidate set carried one study offset per posterior draw**, shared by every candidate, so only the child part of the sum was reweighted by the visit-1 likelihood and the predictive was shrunk toward zero more than the model implies. Each candidate now carries its own study offset, which makes the candidate set the exact per-child prior of the quantity one visit identifies. VG20's second-visit comprehension coverage moved from 0.721 to 0.750 and spoken from 0.837 to 0.856; the earlier version's log-predictive comparison also applied the child correlation to the combined study and child effect, which overstates it, and is superseded by the table above.
6. **A post-hoc banding reported as if it were the test.** The 0.88 spread against a null maximum of 0.58 was one of several bandings and the one that isolated the most extreme twenty rows. The others give 0.54, 0.54 and 0.18, and the note now reports all of them with their own nulls and a statistic that does not depend on the choice. **Where a statistic depends on a cut made after seeing the data, report the cut's alternatives with it.**
7. **The "grows with age" description.** Four band estimates read as a line. The month profile is a step and the within-child change is 0.22; the earlier wording claimed a 0.9-logit slope from 17 to 34 months that no child in the study exhibited.

## Caveats

- One study is one draw from the between-study distribution. Every row shares its study effect, so the 287 rows are not 287 independent tests. The simulated null distributions are the reference for every statistic here, not a binomial standard error and not the profile interval.
- The offsets are point estimates at the posterior mean of the curve and the scales. The profile interval covers the offset's own uncertainty, not the curve's, and treats repeat visits as independent rows.
- The simulated studies are censored at the form's 396 items, as the real one is; on average 15 of 287 simulated rows were censored, against two real rows at the ceiling. That is itself part of the departure: the model expects more of this cohort at the ceiling than there are.
- The three rows from the structurally distinct older sub-sample (62–80 months, near the form ceiling) survive the exclusion and fall in no age band. Too few to matter, but not from the same recruitment as the rest.
- Six models were scored. VG05, VG07 and VG08 were not: they carry no child effect on the conversion ratio, so the within-child test is vacuous for them and they would add nothing the others do not show. VG14 and VG15 were not scored: the study records no signed vocabulary, so only two of their three outcomes could be evaluated, and VG15 does not store the plot-grid variables the script reads. VG16 is deliberately excluded: its conversion predictor is a function of the child's own earlier comprehension, so predicting an unseen study means simulating the lag chain rather than evaluating a likelihood, the same property that excludes it from parameter recovery.
- The offset profile integrates the child effect as a single Gaussian at the group's median age. For VG19 and VG22 that scale is itself age-varying, so their offsets are approximations and their likelihood ratios are withheld; the band profile is what the structural conclusion rests on, and it is unchanged across all five random-effects models.
- Scoring more models against the same study adds evidence about **model structure**, not about the study. The comprehension finding rests on one cohort however many models are scored against it.
